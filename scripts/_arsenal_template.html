The Arsenal

Behind every alert in this dataset is a physical object: a pipe rocket welded in a Gaza workshop, a Soviet-designed Katyusha, a Shahed drone droning at 185 km/h, or a six-tonne ballistic missile arriving from Iran at two kilometres per second. This is a field guide to all of them — what they are, how far they fly, what they carry, and what it costs to shoot them down. A $500 Qassam can force a $50,000 interceptor shot; Iran's June 2025 barrage cost it an estimated $1–6 billion, and defending against it cost more.

The Duel

Fire one. Then fire eighty.

Pick a system and launch it at the deepest target it can reach — watch the trajectory, the interceptor rising to meet it, and the bill both sides run up. Then stack the salvo: interception rates that look unbeatable at ×1 start leaking at ×80, which is the whole logic of saturation barrages.

Illustrative simulation, not a model: intercept rates are anchored to publicly reported figures (~90%+ single-threat claims, ~86–90% across the June 2025 barrages), costs to the documented estimates above — * marks class-level cost estimates. Trajectories and timings are schematic; real flight times shown in the readout.

Method

About this page

This is curated editorial reference material, compiled July 2026 from public sources — CSIS Missile Threat, IISS, the US Defense Intelligence Agency, Wikipedia, and press reporting (each entry links its sources). Specifications for these systems are approximate: ranges and warhead figures are the commonly cited envelopes, and sources frequently disagree — where they do, the entry says so. Unlike the rest of this site, none of this derives from the alert dataset; the raw file is arsenal.json.
